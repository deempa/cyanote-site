A notes app for Windows that never asks you to sign in

By Cyanote · 30 September 2026 · 9 min read
  • Notes
  • Windows
  • Local-first
  • Buying advice

A new Windows PC asks for a Microsoft account before you have seen the desktop, and a good deal of what comes after it assumes you said yes. So when you open something to write down a phone number, a fair question is whether this bit, at least, can stay on the machine: no sign-in, no OneDrive, no subscription, notes in a file on your own disk.

The short answer is yes, and you may already have the tool. The longer answer depends on what you are writing and how much of it there is, so let me go through what Windows ships with first, checked against Microsoft's own pages, before I get to the thing I make.

What Windows already gives you

Notepad

Notepad is better than its reputation. It has had tabs for a while now, and it restores your session — previously open tabs, including unsaved text in them — when you reopen it, which you can switch off in its settings. Microsoft has also been testing Markdown-style formatting — bold, lists, headings, links — with Windows Insiders.

It needs no account to open, type and save. Files are ordinary .txt files wherever you put them, which is the most durable format there is.

The account question arrives with the AI features. Notepad now has Write, Rewrite and Summarize, and Microsoft's support page says that to use them, you need to sign in with your Microsoft account; the subscription versions run through an online Azure service and draw on monthly AI credits. There is a carve-out: on Copilot+ PCs, Microsoft says the features also run on a local model if you are not signed in or have no subscription, in English only. And the same support page says you can turn the AI features off in Notepad's settings. So Notepad is still a no-account editor unless you ask it to be something else.

What it is not is a notes app. There is no list of notes, no folders, no search across everything you have written — you have files, and Windows' file search. At a dozen notes that is fine. At a few hundred, finding last March's thing means remembering what you called it.

Sticky Notes

Sticky Notes is the one people assume needs an account, and it does not. Microsoft's own page is plain about it: on Windows 10 and 11 you don't need to be signed in to your Microsoft account to start using it.

Signing in is what makes them travel. With a Microsoft account, the same page says your notes appear in OneNote on your phone, in Outlook on the desktop and on the web. That is the design: sticky notes are local until you sign in, and then they live in Microsoft's cloud alongside everything else on that account.

For what the name says — a reminder stuck to the screen, a code you need for the next ten minutes — it is exactly right, and I would not replace it. It is not where you keep a year of meeting notes or a project's worth of reference.

OneNote

OneNote is the serious one, and the one with the most history to untangle.

There used to be two OneNotes on Windows. The Windows 10 app was cloud-only, and Microsoft ended its support on 14 October 2025: it is now read-only, for viewing and exporting what is already in it. What remains is the desktop app, now just called OneNote — the descendant of OneNote 2016 — and Microsoft says it is free to download and use, and that it is the only version that supports local notebook storage on your PC's hard drive as well as the cloud.

So local OneNote notebooks do exist. When you create a notebook you choose between OneDrive and This PC. But read the same page and it is clear which way you are being steered: Microsoft says it is best to create notebooks on OneDrive or another shared location. The app is built around the account, and a local notebook is the option you have to go looking for.

If you already live in Microsoft 365, none of that is a cost — OneNote is included, your notebooks follow you, and it is very good at free-form pages, handwriting and clipped screenshots. If you are thinking of leaving it, the notebook format is the real switching cost, and I have written about that separately.

And the clipboard

Windows has clipboard history too, behind Win+V. It is useful and underused. Microsoft's page sets out the limits: 25 entries, cleared each time you restart except for anything you pinned, and syncing between devices is tied to your Microsoft or work account. For "paste the thing I copied a minute ago", that is enough. Where it stops is its own piece: clipboard history on Windows.

Here is where I would stop, if I were you, and not buy anything:

  • You write short things and keep them briefly. Sticky Notes, no account, done.
  • You write longer things, but not many of them. Notepad and a folder. Plain text will outlive every app on this page, and Notepad's session restore covers most of what people mean by autosave.
  • You are already in Microsoft 365 across a phone and two PCs. OneNote. The account is doing real work for you there, and an account-free app would take away sync you actually use. The same trade applies on every platform, and I went through it in a notes app that never asks you to sign up.

Where the built-in tools run out

The gap shows up in a particular kind of person: one PC, a lot of small notes accumulating over years, a to-do list that lives somewhere else, and a clipboard that forgets everything at every restart.

Notepad gives you files but no way to find them. Sticky Notes is the right size for a sticky note and no bigger. OneNote does both, but its centre of gravity is a Microsoft account and OneDrive, and keeping it local means working against its defaults. And none of the three is a to-do list, so tasks end up in yet another app.

None of that is a failing. It is what happens when each tool is designed as part of a set that assumes you signed in at the start.

What I built instead

Cyanote is a desktop app that keeps notes, to-dos and clipboard history in one window, on your own machine. It started on the Mac, and there is now a Windows edition, sold on the Microsoft Store. How the Windows port went, including the parts that did not go well, is written up separately; here is what it does on a PC, and nothing I have not checked.

No account, anywhere. There is no sign-up screen, no email address, no password. You open it and there is a blank note.

Your notes are one file. Everything — notes, tasks, clipboard history — lives in a single SQLite database. By default it sits in a folder called Cyanote in your user folder (C:\Users\<you>\Cyanote), where you can see it, and uninstalling the app leaves it in place. Settings lets you move it to any folder you choose: another drive, or a folder you already back up. Backup in Settings writes everything to a single file you can put anywhere. The general case for this is in what local-first means for your notes, and the practical side in backing up local notes.

It does not talk to a server. The Windows edition has no sync, no cloud and no analytics, and it makes no network requests of its own. The single exception is opt-in: if you subscribe its calendar to a remote iCal address, it fetches that calendar, read-only. Links in your notes open in your browser, which is your browser's business. Pull the network cable and nothing changes.

The clipboard remembers. Clipboard history runs system-wide, keeps entries across restarts, and is searchable. Pick an entry and it pastes straight back into the app you were using. It also respects the flags Windows password managers set on copied secrets, so a password you copy from one is not written into the history — what your clipboard should not remember explains why that matters more than it sounds.

The rest of what a PC app should do. Global keyboard shortcuts open the app and the clipboard from anywhere. Reminders on your to-dos arrive as Windows notifications. It can launch when you sign in to Windows — off until you turn it on. And it registers as an editor for .txt and Markdown files, so "Open with Cyanote" on a text file puts it in a note.

Those are the parts I have tested on a real Windows laptop, not just compiled: clipboard history, paste-back, shortcuts, reminders, launch at login and opening .txt files.

What it costs, and what it does not do

$7.99, once, on the Microsoft Store. No subscription and no tiers; the reasons are in note apps without a subscription. It needs Windows 10 or later.

It is a separate purchase from the Mac version, which is sold directly. Buying one does not include the other.

The limits, stated as plainly as the features. There is no sync: a note written on this PC is on this PC. There is no phone app, no web version and no sharing with other people. If you need your notes on a phone, OneNote or Sticky Notes with a Microsoft account will serve you better, and there is no point pretending otherwise. If what you have is an iPhone full of Apple Notes and a Windows PC at work, that is a different problem with its own answers.

It also does not do what OneNote does best. There is no free-form canvas and no handwriting. Notes are documents, top to bottom, with headings, lists, tables, code blocks and sub-pages.

Choosing

If Sticky Notes or Notepad covers it, keep using them. They cost nothing, ask for nothing, and Microsoft is still improving Notepad in public. If you are in Microsoft 365 on several devices, OneNote is doing a job an account-free app cannot.

If you are on one PC, you want notes, to-dos and a clipboard that survives a restart in one place, and you would rather nobody asked you to sign in to get them, Cyanote is on the Microsoft Store for $7.99, once.
