Clipboard history on Windows

By Lior Rabanian · 30 September 2026 · 9 min read
  • Clipboard
  • Windows
  • How-to

Windows does have a clipboard history, and it is one key combination away: Win + V. The Mac only gained one with macOS 26, tucked inside Spotlight. The catch is in the details. It is off until you switch it on, it keeps 25 items, and it forgets everything that is not pinned when you restart.

For a lot of people that is enough, and they should stop reading here. For the rest, what follows is exactly what Win + V does, where it stops, and when something else earns its place.

How to turn on clipboard history

Press Win + V. The first time, Windows shows a small panel offering to switch the feature on; select Turn on. From then on, everything you copy is added to the list, and Win + V opens it wherever the cursor is. Pick an item and it is pasted into the app you were in.

You can also flip it in Settings → System → Clipboard, where the Clipboard history toggle lives alongside the sync option and a Clear button. Microsoft's own page on using the clipboard covers both routes, and it applies to Windows 10 and 11 alike.

What Win+V actually keeps

Everything below is from Microsoft's support page, not from memory, because this is the kind of feature that changes quietly between releases.

25 items. The history is limited to 25 copied entries. When a 26th arrives, the oldest unpinned one goes. On a busy afternoon of copying and pasting that is not very long at all — an hour, sometimes less.

Text, HTML and images, up to 4 MB each. The supported formats are text, HTML and bitmap, and each item is capped at 4 MB. A large screenshot can exceed that. Copying a file in File Explorer is not one of those formats either, so the history is not a way to get back a file you copied.

Cleared on restart, except pins. Microsoft's wording is plain: the history is cleared each time you restart your PC, except for the items you have pinned. A Windows update that reboots overnight takes the whole list with it.

Pinning. Open the panel, select the three dots next to an item, and pin it. Pinned items survive both the 25-item churn and restarts. This is the feature that turns Win + V from a short-term memory into somewhere to keep the handful of snippets you paste every week — an address, a sort code, a stock reply.

Delete one, or clear all. Each item has a delete option, and there is a Clear all at the top of the panel. Clearing leaves pinned items in place.

Optional sync through your Microsoft account. Under Settings → System → Clipboard there is a separate toggle for clipboard history across your devices. Microsoft says the sync is tied to your Microsoft account or your work account, and that you can have it sync automatically or only when you choose. It is off unless you turn it on, and it is the one part of this feature worth thinking about before you do.

What Microsoft's page does not describe is a way to search the history. At 25 items that mostly does not matter: you scroll. It matters a great deal the day you want something from last Tuesday, because last Tuesday is not in there.

"Win+V is not working"

This is most of the search traffic on the subject, and it usually comes down to one of four things.

It was never switched on. Pressing Win + V on a fresh install shows the panel with a Turn on button and an empty list. Anything you copied before you pressed it was never recorded, which is why the history looks broken the first time rather than empty.

It was switched on after the thing you wanted. Same cause, same answer. Clipboard history cannot reach back before it existed.

You restarted. Everything unpinned is gone after a reboot. That is the design, not a fault, and nothing in Windows recovers it.

Your organisation turned it off. On a work machine, if the toggle in Settings is greyed out with a note that some settings are managed by your organisation, IT has set the Allow Clipboard History policy. When it is disabled, the panel opens and shows nothing. There is a matching policy for the cross-device sync, Allow Clipboard synchronization across devices, which is often switched off on its own. Installing a third-party clipboard manager on that machine is the same conversation with the same people, and I would have it before rather than after.

There is one more case that looks like a fault and is not. An app can say that what it just copied is not allowed in the clipboard history — Microsoft documents the flag as IsAllowedInHistory — and password managers are exactly the apps that should. If a password you copied never appears in Win + V, that is most likely the feature working as intended. What your clipboard should not remember goes into how the convention works and how to test it.

Where Win+V stops

None of this makes Win + V bad. It is a sensible, conservative feature: small, in memory, and gone at the next restart, which is a reasonable default for a buffer that carries passwords and two-factor codes through it all day. The limits are the design, and they are the same limits that make it safe to leave switched on.

But they add up to a particular shape: a scratch buffer for the last hour, with a few pins. It is not a record. The moments where that shows are specific:

  • You copied a paragraph this morning, then spent the afternoon copying links and figures, and the paragraph scrolled off the end at item 26.
  • The machine restarted for an update overnight, and the reference number you copied yesterday went with it.
  • You remember copying something about a supplier, roughly last week, and would find it instantly if you could type "supplier" into the list.

If none of that sounds familiar, Win + V is the right tool and there is nothing to install. If two or three of them do, you have outgrown it, and that is what clipboard managers exist for. I wrote a longer piece on how to tell whether you have really hit a limit of a free tool, from the Mac side, but the test is the same on Windows.

What a clipboard manager adds

A clipboard manager is a small background app that watches the clipboard and writes what it sees to a database on disk. The differences from Win + V are the whole point:

A history that survives restarts. Stored on disk rather than held in memory, so a reboot is no longer a wipe.

A bigger history. Hundreds of items instead of 25, so the paragraph from this morning is still there this evening.

Search. Type a word and the list narrows. This is the one that changes how you use it: history stops being "undo for copying" and becomes somewhere things can be found.

Its own shortcut. Most use a key combination of their own, so Win + V can stay switched on alongside.

The cost is the other side of the same coin. A history that persists on disk is a record of what went through your clipboard, and that deserves more care than a buffer that empties itself. Three things I would check in any of them: where the history is stored, whether it syncs anywhere, and whether it honours the password-manager flag described above. A persistent, searchable history that ignores that flag is keeping your passwords in plain text.

The free option most Windows users land on is Ditto, which is open source, has been around for many years, keeps its history locally and can search it. If all you want is a longer, searchable history on one PC, try that first.

Frequently asked

How many items does Windows clipboard history keep?

  1. Older items are removed as new ones arrive, unless they are pinned.

Does clipboard history survive a restart?

Only pinned items. Everything else is cleared each time the PC restarts.

Can I get back something I copied before I turned Win+V on?

No. Windows only records copies made after clipboard history is switched on. Nothing earlier was ever stored.

Does Win+V sync my clipboard to the cloud?

Only if you turn on the cross-device sync in Settings, and it uses your Microsoft or work account. Clipboard history itself works without it.

Is Win+V the same on Windows 10 and Windows 11?

Microsoft documents the same feature for both: turn it on with Win + V, 25 items, pins, and optional sync.
