When remembering to write it down is the hard part

By Lior Rabanian · 17 August 2026 · 5 min read
  • Method
  • Workflow
  • Accessibility

Nearly all advice about organisation assumes a working memory that holds an intention until you are somewhere you can act on it. Write it down later. Process your inbox at the end of the day. Remember to check the list.

For a lot of people that assumption does not hold, and the result is a persistent, unfair sense of being bad at systems — when what has actually happened is that every system on offer was designed around a capability that varies enormously between people, and nobody said so.

If your experience is that the intention evaporates between having it and reaching somewhere to record it, none of the standard advice is addressing your problem. This is about the version that does.

The principle: never depend on a second step

Every gap between the thought and the record is a place it can disappear. Standard advice puts several of them in a row: have the thought, hold it, get to the app, decide where it goes, write it, later process it into the right place.

That is five opportunities to lose it, and the advice implicitly assumes the loss rate at each is near zero.

Design instead so that a thought recorded once is done. No processing step, no filing decision, no "I will sort that later" — because later is exactly the thing that does not reliably arrive.

What that means concretely

One destination, no decision. Everything goes to the same place. A daily note, an inbox note, one list. Not because it is tidy, but because a choice about where is a step, and steps are where things are lost. Search will find it later; that is what search is for, and it is why filing matters less than people think.

Capture reachable from anywhere. A global shortcut, not an app you switch to. If recording something requires finding a window, the window is a step.

Instant. Under a second. A loading spinner is long enough for the thought to go, and this is not hyperbole — it is the actual failure.

Nothing to process. Systems requiring an inbox to be emptied on a schedule fail here specifically, because the processing session is a recurring appointment with your future attention and it is the first thing to be missed. A system that works without processing is worth more than a better-organised one that requires it.

No decisions at capture time. No category, no priority, no project, no tags. Type the sentence, press return. Anything the app can extract from the sentence itself — a date, a time, a priority — is free; anything requiring a separate choice is a step.

Pull, not push, for retrieval

The other half, and it is where most systems fail people quietly.

Notifications are the standard answer to "how will you remember", and they work poorly here: they arrive when you cannot act, get dismissed reflexively, and the dismissal is complete — no trace, no queue, nothing to come back to. A reminder that fires while you are driving has not reminded you of anything.

What works better is one place you go, containing everything currently relevant, so that arriving is enough and no interruption has to survive. Today's list, checked at a moment that already exists in your day — after coffee, when you sit down, before you leave. Attaching it to something that already happens is the whole trick, because an attached habit survives and a new ritual does not.

Externalise the sequence too

If holding a multi-step task in your head while performing it is unreliable, the fix is not to try harder. It is to write the steps down and read them.

That is what a routine is — six items in order, read rather than recalled. It is not a compensation for a deficiency; it is what aviation and surgery do, for the same reason, with people whose memory is fine.

The list of things you do every time you leave the house. The sequence for the recurring job that always loses a step. The morning order that gets scrambled when something interrupts it. Writing them down converts a memory task into a reading task, and reading is much more robust to interruption.

Redundancy is not untidy

A rule from standard advice that is worth dropping: everything in exactly one place.

If writing it twice makes it more likely to be found, write it twice. If the important date is in the calendar and the task list and on a note stuck to the monitor, that is three chances, and three chances is the point. The cost of duplication is mild untidiness; the cost of a single point of failure is a missed thing.

Optimise for the thing being found, not for the system being elegant.

What tends not to work

Said plainly, so it can be stopped rather than repeated.

Elaborate systems. Anything with more than about two rules. Every rule is a thing to remember about the system, on top of the things you were trying to remember with it.

Inbox processing. Any design where capture is provisional and a later session makes it real.

Reminder escalation. More alerts, louder, more often. Produces dismissal reflex faster than it produces action.

Blaming yourself for the last one failing. Six abandoned systems is information about the systems. It is very commonly read as information about the person, and that reading is what makes people stop trying.

The honest version

I am describing a design pattern, not giving anyone advice about their brain, and there is no software that fixes this. What software can do is remove steps — and since the number of steps is the whole variable, that is not a small contribution.

The two changes that do the most: one destination with no decision at capture, and one place you go, rather than notifications that come to you. Almost everything else is optional.

Cyanote is shaped that way, though not deliberately for this: ⌘N and ⇧⌘D reach a note in one keystroke, tasks take their date and priority out of the sentence so there is nothing else to fill in, ⌥⇧C brings the window up from any app, and Today is one view holding what is scheduled, what is due, which habits are outstanding and which routines have not been run. There is no inbox to process and no filing required — full-text search across everything is what makes that viable.
