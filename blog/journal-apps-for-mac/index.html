The best journal app for Mac, one pick per need

By Cyanote · 23 November 2026 · 11 min read
  • Notes
  • Privacy
  • Buying advice

The best journal app for Mac is Apple Journal for most people, and it's already there if you run macOS 26 or later. It's free, it locks with Touch ID, and its iCloud copy is end-to-end encrypted by default if your Apple Account uses two-factor authentication. Choose Day One for Android, Windows or years of photos. Choose Diarium to pay once and sync through your own cloud. Choose Obsidian for plain files.

I make Cyanote, a notes app that some people keep a journal in. It isn't a journal app and it doesn't win this comparison, so it gets a short section near the end. The rest comes from each developer's own pages and App Store listings on 23 November 2026, not from long-term use.

The short version

  • Best free pick: Apple Journal. Its weak spot is getting entries out: the Mac version exports PDF.
  • Best for photos, prompts and every device: Day One. Free on one device, $49.99 a year to sync.
  • Best without a subscription: Diarium. $14.99 once on the Mac, and a separate purchase on iPhone.
  • Best Apple-only upgrade: Everlog. Free without sync, and it exports every format on the free plan.
  • Best for plain files: Obsidian daily notes. Free, stored as Markdown in a folder.
  • Best beside your tasks, on one Mac: Cyanote, $7.99 once. No prompts, no photo timeline, no phone app.

Older round-ups still say Apple's Journal isn't available on the Mac, or quote a Day One plan called Premium. Both are out of date: Apple publishes a Journal guide for Mac covering macOS 26 and macOS 27, and Day One renamed Premium to Silver in March 2026.

Apple Journal: the one to try first

Apple Journal on the Mac does more than it did when it was an iPhone-only app. You can keep several journals, add photos, video, drawings, audio and locations, see entries on a map, and track a writing streak with scheduled reminders. On macOS 27 it can also suggest writing prompts with Apple Intelligence, based on what you've written, in the languages and regions Apple supports. If you'd rather it didn't, turn off Get Writing Prompts in Journal → Settings → General. Apple's macOS 26 version of the same guide doesn't mention prompts.

Privacy is the strong part. Apple's iCloud data security overview lists Journal data as end-to-end encrypted under standard data protection, which is the default, and says end-to-end encryption needs two-factor authentication on your Apple Account. Ordinary notes in Apple Notes aren't: they only get that if you turn on Advanced Data Protection or lock the note. So for a diary that syncs, Journal is the safer of Apple's two apps out of the box. The other side of that is in the same overview: Apple doesn't have the keys, and can't help you recover the data if you lose access to your account.

To lock it, choose Journal → Settings → General and turn on Lock Journal. The steps are the same on both macOS versions.

Backups and export are the weak part. Apple says Journal entries aren't included in iCloud backups, and tells you to use Time Machine. Encrypt that disk. Sync isn't a backup either, since changes travel to every device. On the Mac, the export guide describes PDF only. The iPhone can do better: Settings → Apps → Journal → Export All Journal Entries saves a ZIP with your photos, locations and other media. If you have an iPhone, make that ZIP twice a year, and store it somewhere encrypted: it's an ordinary file, outside Journal's lock.

Pick it if you only use Apple devices. There's no Android, Windows or web version in Apple's guides.

Day One: the fullest journal, for a yearly fee

On This Day brings back old entries, and there are prompts, templates, streaks, and calendar, map and media views. All of those are on the free Basic plan, along with unlimited entries, a passcode lock and end-to-end encryption. Basic's limits are one photo per entry and one device, though the web app doesn't count as a device.

Silver, at $49.99 a year, raises that to 30 media items per entry and adds sync across all your devices. Gold, at $74.99, adds AI summaries and chat. The pricing guide says monthly plans aren't offered. The App Store listing also shows $8.99 Silver items with no term given, so go by what the app shows you.

Three limits from its encryption FAQ matter:

  • Sync is what's end-to-end encrypted. JSON exports, text backups and Time Machine copies aren't encrypted by Day One, so check that your backup disk is.
  • Old journals may not be covered. End-to-end is the default for new journals, but an older Standard Encrypted one can't be converted. You have to make a new journal and copy the entries across.
  • Lose the key and the synced journal is gone. Day One can't recover it. The key is kept in your iCloud unless you turn that off, so save your own copy before you do.

Pick it if photos are half the entry, or you need Android or Windows. If you already use it and you're wondering whether to go, I covered that in leaving Day One.

Diarium: pay once, use your own cloud

Diarium is the pick if you don't want a subscription or a company holding the entries. It needs no account, runs on Windows, Android, iPhone, iPad and Mac, and Pro syncs through iCloud, OneDrive, Google Drive, Dropbox or WebDAV. It imports from Apple Journal and Day One, and has a map, a timeline and "On this day".

The free version leaves out sync and limits attachments. Pro is $14.99 once on the Mac, with a 7-day evaluation, and the iPhone and iPad version lists its own $14.99 Pro purchase. So Mac plus iPhone is $29.98, which is less than one year of Day One Silver.

Where it falls short: its listing and site describe a lock, and neither claims end-to-end encryption. Your synced copy is as private as the cloud account you put it in. It needs macOS 14.4 or later.

Everlog: a closer match to Apple Journal

Everlog is an app for iPhone, iPad and Mac with no account. Entries stay on your devices and in your iCloud, unless you turn on its optional AI assistants: entries they write pass through an encrypted relay first. The free plan includes unlimited entries and photos, a lock, On This Day, encrypted backups and export in every format. Premium adds iCloud sync, more than one journal, video and audio.

Its comparison page is fair about the trade: iCloud sync is end-to-end encrypted only if you've turned on Advanced Data Protection, while Apple Journal's is by default. The app's release notes mention a Cloud Encryption switch in its iCloud settings too, so check both.

Prices are the awkward part. The App Store listing shows Premium Monthly at $3.99, Everlog Premium at $29.99, and Premium Lifetime at both $59.99 and $29.99. Which one you get is decided inside the app, so look before you buy. Family Sharing is included.

Free and local: Obsidian, jrnl and Apple Notes

Obsidian has a Daily notes core plugin that opens a note for today's date, with a template if you want one. Your entries are Markdown files in a folder, so there's nothing to export. Nothing on those pages describes a lock or encryption for the local files, so FileVault is doing that job. Obsidian Sync, at $4 a month billed yearly, is end-to-end encrypted.

jrnl is for people who like the Terminal. It's free, and one command encrypts the journal file with a password. Lose that password and the journal is gone for good. Use version 4.6 or later: the August 2026 release fixed a security issue.

Mini Diary still appears on lists of free encrypted diaries. Its repository was archived in July 2021, and its README tells people to use something else.

Apple Notes is fine for a diary of words. It can export a note as PDF or Markdown, which Journal's Mac guide doesn't offer. Lock the entries that matter, for the reasons in are Apple Notes encrypted?

What three years costs

A journal is kept for years, so the yearly price adds up. But so does the cost of leaving. Before you pay, write one entry with a photo, export it, and open the file in something else. If it doesn't survive that, the app is holding your diary.

Writing a journal in Cyanote

This one is mine. Cyanote is a notes app, and it suits a written log rather than a scrapbook. ⇧⌘D opens today's dated note, the daily note template has sections for focus, schedule, notes and a log, and right-clicking a day in the calendar opens that day's note. Any note can be locked with a password and is then encrypted with AES-GCM. A locked note's title stays readable, which for a daily note is only the date. Everything sits in one database file on your Mac, with no account and no sync.

It has no prompts, no On This Day, no photo timeline, no weather and no phone app, and unlocked notes aren't encrypted separately, so keep FileVault on. If you forget a note's password, I can't recover it. If a journal is all you want, Apple Journal is free and better at it. Cyanote makes sense if the entries belong next to your tasks and calendar. It's $7.99 once on the Mac, and a separate $7.99 on the Microsoft Store.

Questions people ask

Can I use Apple Journal on an older Mac?

Apple's Mac guide covers macOS 26 Tahoe and macOS 27. On an earlier version, Diarium needs macOS 14.4 and Everlog lists macOS 12.5. Day One's current Mac version needs macOS 15.

Which Mac journal app is the most private?

For a journal that syncs, Apple Journal and Day One both encrypt end-to-end, on Day One by default for new journals. For one that never leaves the Mac, use a local app such as jrnl with encryption on, with FileVault underneath. A lock screen alone hides entries without encrypting them.

Which Mac journal app has no subscription?

Apple Journal is free. Diarium Pro is $14.99 once on the Mac, Everlog sells a one-time Premium, and Obsidian is free without Sync. Day One's paid plans are yearly.
