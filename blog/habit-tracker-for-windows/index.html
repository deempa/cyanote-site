Habit trackers for Windows that are really Windows apps

By Cyanote · 23 November 2026 · 12 min read
  • Habits
  • Windows
  • Buying advice

For most people who want a habit tracker for Windows, start with OpenHabitTracker. It's free and open source, it installs from the Microsoft Store, and the app has no account. If you also want to tick habits on your phone, get Habitify, which has its own Windows app and costs nothing for up to 3 habits. If habits belong next to your tasks, TickTick includes 5 for free. Some other results are Android apps in an emulator.

I make Cyanote, a notes, tasks and habits app sold on the Microsoft Store, so I'm a competitor to everything here. Two of the free apps beat it if habits are all you want. This comes from each developer's own pages and Store listings on 23 November 2026. I haven't used them all side by side.

The short version

  • Free, offline, no account: OpenHabitTracker, or Table Habit if you like a score instead of a streak. Both are on the Microsoft Store.
  • PC and phone together: Habitify. Free for 3 habits, $59.99 once for unlimited.
  • Habits beside a full to-do list: TickTick. Free for 5 habits, then $49.99 a year.
  • In a browser tab: Everyday (3 habits free) or Habitica (free, and a game).
  • Nothing to install: an Excel checkbox grid or a repeating task in Microsoft To Do.
  • Beside your notes on one PC: Cyanote, $7.99 once. It has no phone app and no sync.

Check whether it's a Windows app first

Search for "habit tracker app for PC" and you can land on a BlueStacks page. The one for an app called Habit Tracker is titled "Download and run Habit Tracker on PC & Mac (Emulator)". That's a phone app, running in software that imitates a phone. It works, but it's a lot of software for one tick box.

Loop Habit Tracker is free and open source, but its README calls it "a mobile app", and it's distributed through Google Play and F-Droid. Keep it on your Android phone. OpenHabitTracker and Table Habit both import Loop's data, so you can start on Loop and move to the PC later.

The other old route is gone. Microsoft says the Windows Subsystem for Android and the Amazon Appstore left the Microsoft Store on 5 March 2025, so ignore guides that use them.

Pick by where you'll tick the box

OpenHabitTracker: the free default

OpenHabitTracker's README says it has "no ads, no trackers, no account, no subscriptions and no in-app purchases", and that your data stays on your device unless you sync it to a server you run. It doesn't lead with streaks. Instead it shows how overdue a habit is: one with a 10-day interval that's 2 days late reads 120%. Missing a day "never resets anything", its Store listing says, and ordinary streaks are there if you turn them on.

You can fill in a missed day, and export to JSON, YAML, TSV or Markdown. It imports from Loop, HabitNow, Habitica and Habitify, and has notes and tasks. Its site says the daily summary of what's due arrives on Windows even when the app is closed. There are Android and iPhone versions too, but syncing them means hosting a Docker image yourself.

Table Habit: a score instead of a streak

Table Habit is also free and open source, with no account and no telemetry, and "fully functional" offline by its own description. Each habit builds a score that rises and falls with your consistency, drawn as a growth curve. It also runs on Android and iPhone, and syncs through WebDAV storage you supply, such as Nextcloud or Koofr. If you're willing to set that up, it's the free way to have habits on a PC and a phone without the app's own account. It exports to JSON. Its user guide is written from the Android version, and the changelog shows Windows reminders arriving only in version 1.22.6, so test one on your PC before you depend on it.

Habitify: when the phone matters too

Habitify's download page lists Windows alongside macOS, iOS and Android, and the Windows version is on the Microsoft Store. The free plan covers 3 habits with cross-platform sync, and $59.99 once removes the limit. Syncing needs a registered account: a guest account is stored on a single device and doesn't sync. Its privacy policy says your habits are stored on Firebase and Google Cloud. Three is a sensible number to start with anyway, so use the free plan before you pay.

TickTick, Lunatask and the browser apps

TickTick gives you 5 habits on the free plan inside a full task manager, with a habits widget for the Windows desktop. Premium raises that to 299 habits for $49.99 a year, which only makes sense if you want the task features. Its security page says data is held on AWS in the United States and encrypted at rest.

Lunatask combines habits, tasks, mood and a journal, and encrypts what you type on your device before upload, though some task metadata such as dates and status isn't encrypted. Weekly habits need Premium. Its docs say offline use is for "temporary, short-term" spells. I compare it with Cyanote here.

Everyday is a board of coloured squares in a browser tab. Its devices page lists a Mac app but no Windows one, and syncing is on the paid plan. Habitica turns your dailies into a role-playing game, and missing one costs your character health. Either can sit in its own window: in Edge, open the site and choose Settings and more → More tools → Apps → Install this site as an app.

Installing from the Store, or from a file

Where you get the app matters on Windows, because it decides what Windows can tell you about it.

Microsoft's SmartScreen guide for developers says Store apps are signed by a Microsoft certificate and never show a SmartScreen download warning. An unsigned file shows "Windows protected your PC", and you have to choose Run anyway. Even a signed installer can be flagged until enough people have downloaded it. On Windows 11, Smart App Control can block an unsigned file altogether. A warning doesn't prove an app is bad. It means Windows can't vouch for it.

Table Habit is a good example. Its installation page explains that the MSIX on GitHub is self-signed, and walks you through adding its certificate to your PC's Trusted Root store. The page itself warns that trusting a self-signed certificate "always carries some risks". The Scoop route installs a certificate too. Skip both and use the Store version, which avoids the question. The same goes for OpenHabitTracker, where winget install OpenHabitTracker fetches the Store copy.

Read the description as well as the price. "Free" on a Store page can mean free to download: HabitFlow, which stores its data locally, says openly that it's a 30-day trial, then $3 a month, $19 a year or $39 once. Two other small Windows apps are worth a look. habit forge is free, with streaks, heatmaps and Google Drive sync. Lumina is local and works offline with no account, and its listing says the interface is currently in German. Most of these listings, mine included, have only a handful of ratings, so the rating tells you little.

Do it with what you already have

In Excel, a checkbox grid takes three steps. Excel for the web is free with a Microsoft account, and checkboxes have rolled out to the web and to Microsoft 365 desktop builds.

  1. Put your habits down column A, from A2, and the days of the month along row 1, from B1 to AF1.
  2. Select the grid, B2 to AF2 and down, then choose Insert → Checkbox.
  3. In AG2, enter =COUNTIF(B2:AF2,TRUE) and copy it down.

The formula works because Microsoft's checkbox page says a ticked box is the value TRUE and an empty one is FALSE. You get a monthly count. You don't get reminders or a streak, and you'll need a new sheet each month.

In Microsoft To Do, select a task, choose Repeat, and pick daily, weekdays, weekly or a custom repetition. Microsoft has a page on building daily habits this way: a daily repeating task gets suggested for My Day each morning. Neither page mentions a streak or a history grid, so it's a reminder rather than a record. To Do needs a Microsoft account too, as I covered in to-do lists for Windows without an account.

Where your habit history ends up

If you track medication, drinking or mood, the row matters. A local record can't leak from a server it was never on, but it also won't survive a failed SSD unless you copy it somewhere. OpenHabitTracker, Table Habit and Cyanote all export to a file, so put that file somewhere else once a month. My guide to backing up local notes covers where.

Questions people ask

Is there a free habit tracker for Windows 11?

Yes. OpenHabitTracker and Table Habit are both free on the Microsoft Store with no account and no in-app purchases, by their developers' descriptions. Habitify and TickTick have free plans capped at 3 and 5 habits.

Does Windows 11 have a habit tracker built in?

I found no habit tracker among the apps Microsoft documents for Windows. Microsoft To Do's repeating tasks are the closest, and they need a Microsoft account.

Can I use Loop Habit Tracker on a PC?

Not as a Windows app. It's Android software, so on a PC it would need an emulator. You can export your data from Loop and import it into OpenHabitTracker or Table Habit.
