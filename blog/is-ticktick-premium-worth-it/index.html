Is TickTick Premium worth it? Free vs paid at $49.99 a year

By Cyanote · 23 November 2026 · 10 min read
  • Tasks
  • Buying advice
  • Subscriptions

TickTick Premium is worth $49.99 a year if you plan your days on its calendar, build filters, or share a list with more than one other person. Otherwise stay on the free plan, which already includes recurring tasks, subtasks, tags, a Kanban view, a Pomodoro timer, five habits and sync. Use it until a limit stops you, then check whether Premium lifts that one.

I make Cyanote, a $7.99 one-time desktop app with tasks and habits in it, so I compete with a small part of TickTick, which is the stronger task manager by a wide margin. Everything here is a reading of TickTick's own pages and store listings on 23 November 2026, not a long-term test.

The short version

  • The price is $49.99 a year, or $4.99 a month on the App Store. Anything quoting $35.99 or $27.99 is out of date, including part of TickTick's own Mac App Store page.
  • Free stops at 9 lists, 99 tasks per list, 2 reminders per task, 5 habits and one other person on a shared list.
  • Some things are missing on free altogether: filters, task duration, constant reminders, the timeline view and the AI features.
  • I found no free trial. Buy on the web and there's a 14-day refund window. Buy through Apple and the refund is Apple's decision.
  • Five years of Premium is $249.95. Todoist Pro is $300 over the same time. Things 3 is $59.98 once for a Mac and an iPhone.
  • If you stop paying, you keep your data and go back to a free account.

What TickTick Premium costs now

TickTick's upgrade page shows one paid plan: $49.99 a year, with no team or family tier. The Mac and iPhone listings show the same yearly price, plus a monthly option at $4.99. One account covers the Mac, Windows, Linux, iPhone, Android and web versions on its download page.

The older figures were real. An archived copy of the upgrade page from 26 February 2026 shows $35.99, so the yearly price has gone up by $14, or 39%. The $27.99 figure survives in an odd place. The description on TickTick's Mac App Store page still says $2.99 a month or $27.99 a year, while the in-app purchase list on the same page says $4.99 and $49.99. Trust the purchase list, because that's what Apple charges.

Raw size is rarely the reason to pay. Nine lists is enough for work, home, errands and a few projects, and a list with 99 open tasks needs weeding more than it needs room. The limits that bite are structural:

  • Reminders. Two per task means "the day before" and "an hour before" uses up the allowance. Constant Reminder, which keeps notifying you until you act, is Premium only.
  • Habits. Five is a sensible ceiling for most people, and habit apps you can buy once start at less than one year of Premium.
  • Sharing. Free covers a couple. A household of three doesn't fit. Only the list's owner needs to pay, so one Premium account can cover everyone's shared shopping list.

What Premium adds beyond bigger numbers

Planning on a calendar. This is the main reason to buy. TickTick's own pitch for Premium is more calendar views, a start and end time on each task, and subscribed outside calendars. The Google connection is unusually good: per TickTick's guide, events you add, change or delete on either side sync to the other. If you drag tasks into time slots, you need durations, and durations are Premium.

How much calendar you get for free is harder to say. The table only says "Basic". A 2020 post on TickTick's blog said free users got the list view and nothing else, and the Mac listing calls the grid and timeline views Premium. Both are old, so open the calendar on a free account and look.

Filters. A filter is a saved view, such as high-priority work tasks due this week, built from lists, tags, dates and priority with AND and OR. Once you have several hundred open tasks, this stops being a nicety. The free plan has tags and priorities, but no way to save a combination.

A record of changes. List and task activity shows who changed what. It matters on a shared list and hardly at all on your own.

Fuller statistics. The free plan keeps focus and habit records. Premium adds detail, such as a whole month of habit check-ins in one view, on the mobile apps only.

AI features. The assistant, voice capture and recording summaries share a monthly quota, even on Premium. Connecting an outside AI tool through TickTick's MCP server is ticked on both plans, so you don't need Premium for that.

When free is enough

Stay on free if your tasks are a list you work through, you keep nine lists or fewer, and your calendar lives in another app. You get the same capture, recurring tasks, subtasks, list and Kanban views, and sync as paying users. The Pomodoro timer is listed among the standard features too. If that's what drew you, a Pomodoro timer on a Mac covers the standalone options.

Against Todoist's free plan it's a trade. Todoist stops at 5 personal projects where TickTick allows 9 lists, but it lets 5 people share a project and TickTick allows 2. I covered how quickly five projects run out in leaving Todoist.

Trial, refunds and where to buy

I looked for a free trial on the upgrade page, in the help centre and on both App Store listings, and found none mentioned. What you can rely on is the refund policy, and it depends on where you pay:

  1. Web, Windows or Android: refundable within 14 days of payment, requested from Order History in Settings. Cancel the subscription as well, and don't generate an invoice first: TickTick says orders with invoices can't be refunded.
  2. iPhone or Mac App Store: TickTick says it can't refund these, and sends you to Apple.
  3. Gift cards: no refunds.

So if you're unsure, buy on the web, use it hard for ten days and decide. If you bought through Apple, cancel there, because the iPhone listing says an App Store subscription renews unless you turn that off at least 24 hours before the period ends.

What you lose if Premium lapses

Your data stays. TickTick's help page says everything is retained after Premium expires and the account becomes a free one. It doesn't spell out what happens to the part above the limits: your tenth list, a sixth habit, or the filters you built. The February 2026 upgrade page said you keep lists, tasks and images but can't add more until you renew. The current page has dropped that line, so take a backup before you let it lapse.

Where your tasks are stored, and how to export them

TickTick is a cloud service from Appest, the company named in its terms. Its security page says servers and databases are on Amazon Web Services in the United States and that user data is encrypted at rest. The privacy policy, revised 9 July 2026, adds encryption in transit, no sale of personal data, and outside AI providers that handle what you put into the AI features. Neither page mentions end-to-end encryption, so don't assume it. After you delete an account, data stays in backup archives for 90 days. Two-step verification is available, and worth turning on.

For a copy, use the web version: avatar, then Settings > Account > Backup & Import, then Generate Backup. The page doesn't name the format. Two apps that import it, SingularityApp and Mindwtr, describe a CSV file, and Mindwtr warns that attachments may have to be moved by hand. That's an easier exit than Things, whose export page doesn't mention CSV.

If not Premium, then what?

  • Apple Reminders is on your Mac already, and has subtasks, tags and shared lists. If you only use Apple devices, try it before paying anyone.
  • Things 3 if you want a calm list for one person, only on Apple devices, with no yearly bill.
  • Todoist Pro if your colleagues already use it. Its 300 projects are no real gain over TickTick's 299 lists.
  • One-time apps in general: my round-up of Mac to-do apps you can buy once compares seven.

Where Cyanote fits

Cyanote puts tasks, habits, a Pomodoro timer and a calendar in one window next to your notes and clipboard history. It keeps them in one file on your own disk, with no account.

It isn't a TickTick replacement. It has no phone app, no sync and no sharing. Tasks don't have subtasks and can't repeat, and it can show a Google calendar but not write to it. If you add tasks on your phone or share a list, use TickTick.

It makes sense if you do all of this at one desk, want it stored locally, and would rather pay $7.99 once than every year. That's the Mac price. Windows is a separate $7.99 on the Microsoft Store, and one doesn't unlock the other.

Questions people ask

Is TickTick free forever?

The iPhone listing says it's "always free". The free plan has size limits, not a time limit.

Is there a lifetime TickTick licence?

Not on any page I found. TickTick's upgrade page offers a yearly plan, and the App Store purchase lists show monthly and yearly.

Does everyone on a shared list need Premium?

No. TickTick says only the list's owner needs it to share with up to 29 people. The others can take part, but don't get Premium features.

Why do other sites say TickTick costs $35.99?

That was the yearly price in February 2026. TickTick now charges $49.99, and some reviews haven't been updated.
