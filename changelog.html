Changelog

Every release, and what changed in it.

Updates are free, forever. Buy Cyanote once and every version below — and everything after it — is included. The app checks for new releases on its own and updates itself; nothing to re-purchase, no subscription.

1.41.0

7 August 2026Latest

Settings that fit on one screen, fifteen themes to choose from, tables you can take rows out of as easily as you put them in, and a set of ready-made notes and routines that carry the method with them.

If you already have Cyanote, read the first line of the next section and then forget about it.

A licence key, for new copies only

  • Nothing changes for you if you are already running Cyanote. No key, no prompt, no action — not now and not later. Your copy stays exactly as it is.
  • A new install asks for the licence key from your purchase email, once. Paste it on first launch and it is never mentioned again. There is no account, no sign-in, and nothing is checked afterwards — the app does not phone home on launch or on a timer.
  • If the key will not go through, the message says which of the handful of reasons it was: not found, already used on the maximum number of computers, expired. If you are simply offline, it says so and waits — it never throws away what you typed.
  • Settings → General shows the last four characters of the key you used, so you can confirm it took.

Settings you can read

  • Every page now fits on one screen. Appearance ran to nearly two screens of scrolling and is now a single one — with sixteen themes on it rather than nine. Names on the left, controls on the right, the way a system preferences pane does it, instead of a column half the width of the window that wrapped two-word labels onto three lines.
  • Seven new themes: Lagoon, Cobalt, Newsprint, Moss, Ember, Mulberry and Midnight — the last a true black for OLED screens. Each has a full light and dark palette, and every one was checked for readable contrast rather than eyeballed.
  • ⌘, opens Settings, like every other Mac app. It always worked; it was just never listed, so it could not be found or changed.
  • The clipboard shortcuts are visible again. They are listed under Shortcuts alongside everything else. If one cannot be registered, the app now names the shortcut you are looking at and tells you what the system said, instead of a vague failure.
  • The font dropdown listed "System" twice, and picking the second one did nothing.

Tables

  • Rows and columns can be removed. A row could not be deleted at all, and a column only from two clicks inside a menu. Both now sit next to the buttons that add them, and there is a control beside each row for taking that particular row out.
  • Adding a column is one click, next to the button that adds a row.
  • Choosing what a column holds — text, a tick box, a number — is a proper picker now, with the three side by side and the current one marked, rather than a list of words.

Notes and routines that know the method

  • New ready-made notes: a decision journal (what you decided, what you expected to happen, and when to check whether you were right), Cornell notes, a retro, an incident review and reading notes.
  • The weekly review, project brief and brainstorm templates were rewritten around what actually makes them work — the weekly review now follows its three passes, and the project brief asks for non-goals.
  • Ready-made routines, reachable at any time from the Routines page: morning start, deep work, inbox zero, a shutdown ritual and a weekly review. Timeboxes only where the clock is part of the method.
  • Starting a note from a template now opens it. Picking one while you were on Today or the calendar created the note and left you where you were, which looked exactly like nothing happening.

Habits

  • A habit that is not due today can no longer be ticked. The streak only ever counted days a habit was actually due, so ticking a Saturday habit on a Wednesday wrote something that changed nothing. The tick now waits, and says which day it is waiting for.
  • The done button looks like a button. It showed the habit's emoji until you ticked it, which reads as decoration; it is now a tick box that is empty or filled, and the emoji sits next to the name where it belongs.

Smaller things

  • Note icons in the sidebar are all one size — an emoji no longer looks larger than the note above it.
  • Numbers beside the text-size and spacing sliders no longer collide with the slider on wider typefaces.

1.40.0

6 August 2026

Your calendar now says whether it is actually up to date. Tasks understand the way you already write them. The clipboard opens the instant you ask for it, on the tab you asked for. And a lot of things that sat two clicks deep are now in front of you.

Your calendar, at a glance

  • The calendar says whether it is in step with Google. A small marker sits above the week: a green dot and *Synced* when every calendar you follow refreshed cleanly, with the name and how long ago in the tooltip. If one of them could not be reached it turns amber and says *Sync issue* instead of leaving you to wonder. A paused calendar is not treated as a problem — you turned it off on purpose.
  • If you have not connected a calendar yet, it says so and offers to help. The same spot becomes *Sync with Google Calendar*, which opens the panel where you import a file or paste a calendar address — the same one that lived in Settings, now reachable from the calendar you are looking at.
  • Events can repeat. An event window now has *Repeats* — daily, weekly or monthly — and a weekly standup appears every week. Editing or deleting one changes the whole series. A repeating rule that came in from another calendar and is more intricate than those three is left exactly as it was found rather than being flattened.
  • Reminders can arrive before the thing starts. Instead of a single tick box for "remind me at the start", pick five, ten or thirty minutes, an hour, or a day ahead. For a repeating event the reminder follows the next occurrence, and re-arms itself on the one after that, so a series reminds you every time rather than once.
  • Dragging an event to a new time now carries its reminder with it, keeping the same head start, instead of quietly collapsing it to the start time.

The day in front of you

  • Today opens with today's schedule. The dashboard lists what is actually on your calendar today, with the next thing highlighted and counting down — *in 25 min* — so the page answers "what now" and not only "what is outstanding". Events that have already finished are greyed out and struck through, all-day items say so, and a quiet line tells you when the day is clear.

Write a task the way you would say it

  • Type "pay rent tomorrow 9am !high" and the parts come out. The date, the time and the priority are lifted from what you typed, the words themselves leave the task's name, and each guess appears as a small chip you can dismiss with an ✕ if it read you wrong. Weekdays, *today*, *tomorrow*, *next week* and dates like *12 Aug* all work, and so do p1/p2/p3.
  • It stays deliberately cautious: anything it cannot read cleanly is left alone in the text rather than mangled.

Writing

  • Callouts. /callout puts a tinted panel around something that deserves to stand out, with an icon you can swap between 💡, ⚠️, ✅ and ❗.
  • Toggles. /toggle gives you a heading with a triangle that folds the detail underneath out of sight, and remembers whether it was open.
  • Notes tell you what points at them. *Linked from* now sits under the note itself and updates as links change. Before, it hid behind a button and only refreshed when you opened the note, so a rename or a deletion could leave it quietly wrong.
  • Adding a column to a table is as easy as adding a row. A column has its own button beside the row button under every table. Until now a row took one click and a column took two, buried in a menu.
  • Choosing what a column holds is a proper picker. Text, Yes/No and Number are three tiles you can see and compare, each with its own colour, with the current one clearly marked — and the column's own marker in the header now carries that colour, so a glance across the top row tells you which columns are numbers and which are tick boxes.

Habits

  • Starting from nothing gives you something to start from. With no habits yet, the page offers eight ready-made ones — walking, reading, water, meditating, strength, journalling, sleep, tidying — each showing its schedule before you commit. Tick the ones you want and add them together.
  • The new-habit window leads with the name. It opens as the real form with the name field waiting, and one press of Enter with just a name is enough. The ready-made list is no longer a wall in front of the form: it opens when you click the name field, and closes with Escape without disturbing anything you have already filled in.

Focus

  • The timer knows what you are working on. Pick a task to focus on and finished sessions are counted against it, so the page can tell you where the day actually went — *Finish the itinerary — 2 focus sessions*. Renaming or completing the task later leaves that history readable. With nothing picked, the timer behaves exactly as before.

The clipboard

  • It opens faster. The database it reads is made ready in the background shortly after launch rather than on your first press, the first screenful is smaller so less has to travel before anything appears, and the list is no longer fetched twice on every open.
  • The pinned snippets shortcut opens on pinned snippets. It used to show the ordinary history for a fraction of a second and then switch, because the window was put on screen before it had been told which tab you asked for.

Bringing notes in

  • Import Markdown files. Settings → Backup → *Import Markdown files* takes .md files and turns them into notes — headings, lists, tick boxes, quotes, code blocks and links all survive. Anything it does not understand is kept as plain text rather than dropped, and importing the same file twice does not give you two copies of it.

Smaller things

  • The sidebar reads properly to a screen reader: every section is named, and the page you are on is announced as the current one.
  • Note icons in the sidebar are all one size now — a note with an emoji no longer looks bigger than the one above it.

1.39.0

5 August 2026

Your Google Calendar can come with you. No more licence key — updates simply work. Reminders that quietly never arrived now arrive, and on time. And your notes stop disappearing from the sidebar the moment you open anything else.

Bring your Google Calendar in

  • Import a calendar file. Settings → Calendar → *Choose .ics file*. Export a calendar from Google (or anywhere else that speaks the standard format) and it lands in Cyanote as ordinary events you own and can edit. Importing the same file twice will not double anything up.
  • Or subscribe to a calendar and let it keep itself current. Paste the calendar's private address — in Google Calendar that is Settings → the calendar → *Secret address in iCal format* — and Cyanote re-checks it every half hour. Each subscribed calendar gets its own colour and shows when it last refreshed.
  • Repeating events now show every time they happen. A weekly standup appears every week, not once. Events you excluded from a series stay excluded.
  • All-day events sit in the all-day strip across the days they cover, rather than as a block in the hour grid — and a three-day event covers three days, not four.
  • Two things worth knowing, and the app says both where you need them. Subscriptions are read-only: events you create in Cyanote are yours and stay in Cyanote, and are never written back to Google. And Google rebuilds these feeds on its own schedule, so something you added on your phone can take a few hours to appear here — that is Google's cadence, not a fault in Cyanote.
  • Removing a subscription asks whether to keep the events it brought in, so unsubscribing never quietly deletes a chunk of your calendar.
  • That private address grants read access to the whole calendar, so Cyanote treats it like a password: it is stored on your computer with everything else and is only ever sent to the calendar it came from.

The licence key is gone

  • There is no key to enter any more, and nothing to keep safe. Cyanote never checked one to run — it only ever gated downloading updates — so all that field did was stand between you and an update you had already paid for. Updates now just download.
  • If you had a key saved, you can forget it. Nothing needs it and nothing will ask for it again.

Reminders arrive, and arrive on time

  • On Windows, reminders could fail silently and completely. The check for "am I allowed to send notifications" reports *denied* on Windows before anything has asked, and every reminder was quietly dropped on the floor with no error and no way to tell. Reminders no longer ask that question; they send.
  • A reminder set for 09:15 could wait until 09:15:29. The background check ran every thirty seconds from whenever the app happened to start, so it rarely landed on the minute you asked for. It now ticks on the clock, so a reminder set for a given minute is checked at that minute.
  • Reminders were slow to catch up after your machine slept. Closing the lid froze the timer; it now notices the gap on waking and checks immediately.
  • Some reminders could arrive twice. Two separate timers could both spot the same reminder before either had marked it as sent.
  • A habit whose reminder time was saved oddly would nag on every single check, from the moment the app opened, forever. It now reads the time properly, or leaves it alone.
  • Opening the app at eleven at night no longer delivers a reminder for eight that morning. A daily reminder is delivered up to two hours late, and after that it is left alone — and a one-off more than a day stale is dropped silently rather than arriving as a pile of dead notifications after a week away.
  • Settings → a new reminder check tells you why reminders might not be reaching you: whether notifications are permitted, whether the background timer is alive, when the last check ran, what is due next, and whether Cyanote is set to start with your computer.

The sidebar keeps your notes in view

  • Opening the To-Do list, the calendar, habits or anything else used to empty the sidebar. The app stored "which page am I on" and "which notes am I looking at" in the same place, so choosing a page silently meant choosing no notes. Your notes now stay listed on every page, and switching between All notes, Favorites and Recent no longer throws you back to the notes view.
  • Trash gets its own marker in that strip while you are in it, so it is clear what you are looking at.
  • The buttons that expand and collapse the tree no longer appear over flat lists where they did nothing.

1.38.0

3 August 2026

The calendar shows a week at a time now, with the hours down the side and every event sitting where it actually falls. Ticking something off stops making it vanish. And five pages that used a third of the window now use the window.

A calendar you can plan on

  • The calendar opens on the week, as a proper time grid — hours down the left, days across the top, and each event drawn at the time it happens and the height its length deserves. Until now a week was seven lists of text, where a five-minute call and an all-afternoon meeting looked exactly alike.
  • Click an empty slot and start typing. A one-hour event appears at that time with the caret already in its name, so a new event costs one click and a word. Enter keeps it, Escape throws it away. Drag down the column instead of clicking and the event takes the length you drew.
  • A small month calendar sits to the right of the grid. Today is circled, the days you are looking at are shaded, and clicking any date takes the main view there. It steps out of the way on a narrow window so the week never gets squeezed.
  • Drag an event to move it — up and down to change the time, across to change the day, both in one gesture. Times are snapped to the quarter hour, and the event's own label counts along as you drag, so you can see you have landed on 13:45 and not 13:30.
  • Drag an event's top or bottom edge to make it longer or shorter. The other end stays put.
  • Events that overlap now sit side by side. Previously they were drawn on top of one another, so the shorter one was simply invisible.
  • The panel that took the right-hand third of the page is gone. Tasks due and habits due moved to an all-day strip across the top, where a calendar keeps that sort of thing, and each day's header carries its own button for that day's note.
  • Day, Week, Month and Agenda are all still there, and the week is what you get on opening.
  • An event can be deleted from the event window. Delete used to live only on the right-click menu, which is not somewhere you would think to look.

Finishing something no longer throws it away

  • Ticking a task off keeps it. On the To-Do page, on the Today card and on the Board, a finished task now stays visible — struck through, under a *Done* divider, below the work that is still open — and leaves only when you delete it yourself.
  • On the Today card this was at its worst: completing a task removed the row outright, so ticking it off and deleting it looked identical.
  • Un-tick to bring a task back. It returns to the open list in its original place.
  • "Clear completed" asks first. It permanently deletes every finished task, and until now it did that on one click with nothing in between.
  • Board cards have a delete button — a small bin in the bottom-right corner of the card, and *Delete* on the right-click menu. Either way it can be undone from the message that follows.

Room to work

  • To-Do, Routines, Habits and Today used a narrow strip down the middle of the window. They now run to the full width, and the space goes into fitting more on screen rather than stretching what was already there: habits as a grid of cards, routines side by side, and Today's sections in columns. Pomodoro keeps its single centred column — a timer is something you look at, not something to spread out.
  • The sidebar's buttons no longer come apart when you narrow it. "New note" used to wrap onto two lines with its icon crushed into the corner. The buttons now drop their labels and become evenly sized icons before there is any risk of that, and the labels stay available as tooltips.

Fixes

  • Deleting a note threw you to the top of the list. Whatever you deleted, you landed on the first note in the tree — because deleting never chose a neighbour at all, it just fell back to the oldest open tab, which after any normal session is the note the app opened at startup. It now selects the note that takes the deleted one's place: the next one down, or the one above if you deleted the last, or the parent if it was an only child.
  • Emptying the Trash left tabs pointing at notes that no longer existed.

Linked code notes

  • A code note linked to a file now knows when it has drifted from it. Editing the note never wrote to the file — saving is deliberate — so the two parted company the moment you typed. Unlinking or trashing the note then dropped those edits without a word. It now warns you first, and says which notes are affected.

1.37.0

3 August 2026

Fonts go where you expect them to, twenty tools become seven, adding a habit is one screen instead of two, and there is no longer any text in the app you cannot read.

Picking a font for a note actually changes the note

  • A font chosen from a note's T menu now takes the note's title and headings with it. Before, those stayed on the theme's display face no matter what you picked, so the biggest text on the page ignored your choice and the setting looked broken.
  • If you would rather headings kept their own face, say so in the note menu or in Settings → Editor → Heading font. An explicit choice still wins; the display face is now the default rather than a lock.
  • Heading font set to Match body now matches *that note's* body font. It was quietly falling back to the app-wide font instead, so a note set to Lora got Inter headings.

A font for the rest of the app

  • New Settings → Appearance → App font, covering the sidebar, tabs, menus, To-Do, Habits, Calendar and Settings itself. Until now the font settings only ever reached note text, which is why changing one seemed to work in some places and not others.
  • It defaults to your system font, so nothing changes unless you pick something. Note text still follows Settings → Editor → Body font — the two are separate on purpose.

Twenty tools became seven

  • The Tools page had grown a drawer of things a notes app has no business holding: a colour picker, a unit converter, a hash generator, a UUID generator, a placeholder-text generator, a number-base converter, a cron reader and IP subnet maths. They are gone. What is left is the seven you reach for while writing or reading something: Text, Compare texts, Encode / decode, JSON, Regex, URL inspector and Timestamp.
  • Text is one tool now instead of four. Word count, change case, sort & dedupe and hidden characters each had their own box, so running two of them on the same paragraph meant pasting it twice. There is one box now: the counts update as you type, every button below works on that same text, and Use as input puts an answer back at the top so operations follow on from each other.
  • Encode / decode is one tool now instead of three. Paste a JWT, a Base64 blob or a percent-encoded string and it works out which it is and decodes it — no picking a format first, and no pressing a button to see the answer. Choose a format yourself and it stops guessing and stays where you put it.
  • Shuffle was not shuffling. The line-shuffle button leaned heavily towards leaving lines close to where they started, so a shuffled list was not a random one. It now uses a real shuffle, and every ordering is equally likely.
  • The "find a tool" box is gone too. It was there because twenty entries were too many to scan; seven fit on screen at once.

Adding a habit is one screen

  • The new-habit dialog used to open on a list of ready-made habits and swap to a form the instant you picked one, growing 130 pixels taller under your cursor at the exact moment you committed. It is one screen now. The name field is also the search, the ready-made habits hang underneath it, and picking one fills in the icon, colour, category, schedule and reminder in place. Nothing jumps.
  • You can see nine ready-made habits at a time instead of four. The list was capped at a height that kept 87% of the catalogue below the fold, so "Create my own" sat a page and a half down and most people never saw it.
  • Icon, colour and category used to be three labelled sections taking up 260 pixels for decisions the ready-made habit had already made. They are one row under the name now, and the dialog is 40% shorter.
  • When a ready-made habit sets your schedule or reminder, the line it changed is marked, and a note underneath says which habit set it — with an Undo if that was not what you wanted.

Fixes

  • Typing a habit name that was not in the list could create a completely different habit. Typing "run" and pressing Enter created *Practise an instrument*, three times a week at six in the evening. "diet" created *Dinner together*, "tea" created *Take vitamins*. The list matched far too loosely and Enter took whatever happened to be at the top. Enter now goes to the best genuine match, and when nothing really matches it goes to "create your own" — which is what typing "run" meant in the first place.
  • Pressing Escape while typing a habit name threw the name away and left you on a blank form. It keeps what you typed.
  • Naming your own habit after looking at a ready-made one borrowed its appearance — pick "Call family", change your mind, type "Gym", and you got Gym in rose, filed under Social, every Sunday at five. Making your own now starts clean.
  • A habit picked after another one could keep the first one's days. Choose a weekly habit, then a daily one, and the old weekday was still ticked underneath — invisible until you switched to "Certain days" and found it there.
  • The full-screen focus view never opened. The window it lives in had never been granted permission to hear that a session had started, so it sat hidden and starting a focus session appeared to do nothing at all — with no error, because every layer of that path failed quietly. The countdown described in 1.36.0 works now.
  • Regex in Tools could freeze the whole window. A pattern that backtracks badly ran on the same thread as the interface, with nothing to stop it — twenty-six characters took five seconds, thirty-one took over a minute, and it re-ran on every keystroke. It now runs out of the way and gives up after a second, saying so.
  • Base64 failed on anything large, showing a raw error instead of a result. It handles a megabyte, including text that is not English.
  • Comparing two long files could exhaust memory — two twenty-thousand-line files needed 1.5GB. The same comparison now uses 89MB and finishes in a tenth of a second, and says so when it has had to stop early.

Text you can read, in every theme

  • Code blocks in a note were nearly invisible in light mode. The text was a pale grey meant for a dark code block, on a white one. It is now the theme's own ink, in all nine themes.
  • Secondary text was too faint to read in Tomoe and Sepia — dates, word counts, hints and labels. Both have been darkened enough to be legible while staying the warm, quiet themes they are.
  • Every colour the app puts text on top of is now checked against the surface behind it. Habit chips, category labels, the board, routines, the Pomodoro buttons, error messages, reminder chips, toast icons and the sidebar icons were all picking a text colour by a rule of thumb that got it wrong more often than not — ten of the thirteen accent colours were choosing white where black was twice as readable.
  • The keyboard focus ring is properly visible now, on every surface in every theme. It was a half-transparent outline that disappeared against several of them, and it is the only thing telling a keyboard user where they are.
  • Loading placeholders were so faint they were hard to tell from an empty screen.
  • Disabled Back, Forward and page arrows were faint enough to look like the button had gone missing rather than being unavailable.

The clipboard window follows your screen

  • If you move between a laptop screen and a larger display, the clipboard window now opens on the screen you are actually working on, at a size that suits it, instead of staying the size and place it was when the app started. It reads the display under your pointer each time it opens, so plugging in or unplugging a monitor needs no restart.

1.36.0

2 August 2026

Full-screen focus, automatic backups, and a Pomodoro page and habit form that are much easier to read.

Focus mode

  • Starting a focus session now covers the whole screen, over whatever else you had open, leaving just the countdown. Breaks are left alone on purpose — the point of a break is getting your machine back.
  • Esc leaves the full-screen view at any time and the timer keeps running. Pause and End are on screen too, and the way out is always visible rather than something to guess at.

Your notes back themselves up

  • Cyanote now takes a backup once a day, on its own, and keeps the last seven. They live in a backups folder next to your notes, and Settings → Backup lists them by date so you can put one back in a couple of clicks.
  • Clipboard history is left out of these, which is what keeps them small — a few kilobytes rather than tens of megabytes — and restoring one leaves your current clipboard exactly as it is rather than replacing it with last week's.

A calmer Pomodoro page

  • The eight timer settings no longer sit under the clock. They fold into a single line that still shows what they are set to, and open when you want them.
  • The three phase buttons are gone. Instead the page draws the cycle you are actually in — Focus → Break → Focus → Long break — and marks where you are, with a line underneath saying what comes next.
  • Sessions finished today is now a number you can see rather than small grey text, and Reset and Skip say what they do.

Habits are quicker to add

  • The new-habit form opens on a list of ready-made habits grouped by category — water, sleep, reading, deep work and about thirty more — each arriving with its own icon, colour and a sensible schedule. Type to narrow the list, or keep typing to name one of your own.
  • How often and the reminder time are always on show, before and after you create it, and every habit row now has an edit button. Changing a habit later no longer means knowing to right-click it.

Fixes

  • The clipboard could open empty, even with a full history. It now notices the window opening several different ways, and always starts at the top with the newest item ready to paste.
  • Typing in a note made the sidebar flicker. Every keystroke was rebuilding all the navigation icons, for text the sidebar does not even show.
  • Saving a template could say "Saved" when it had not been. A failure now says what happened instead of claiming success.
  • There is room to keep writing below the last line of a note, instead of the caret sitting against the bottom of the window.

1.34.0

27 July 2026

Cyanote is now signed by Apple and runs on every Mac, and the clipboard is much quicker to open.

Please re-allow paste

  • macOS ties the "control your computer" permission to the app's signature, and this release is signed with a new Apple-issued one. The clipboard's paste-back will stop working until you allow Cyanote again in System Settings → Privacy & Security → Accessibility. It is a one-off, and copying and pasting by hand keeps working in the meantime.

Runs everywhere, no warning

  • Cyanote is now notarized by Apple. It opens normally on first launch instead of warning that it is damaged or from an unidentified developer, and there is no need to right-click → Open any more.
  • Builds are universal, so Cyanote runs natively on both Apple Silicon and Intel Macs. Until now the download and the updates were Apple Silicon only — if you are on an Intel Mac, this is the first version you can install.

A faster clipboard

  • The app no longer loads your whole clipboard history at launch. It reads it when you open the clipboard, a page at a time as you scroll, which takes a noticeable chunk off startup — especially if your history holds screenshots.
  • Opening the clipboard always starts at the top of the list again. It used to reopen wherever you had last scrolled to, with the newest item somewhere off-screen above.
  • Searching the clipboard now searches your whole history rather than only the part already loaded.
  • Copying is quicker too: a history full of large images no longer has to be scanned end to end on every copy to spot duplicates.

1.33.0

27 July 2026

A Pomodoro timer, clocks that read the way you want them to, and a long list of repairs to the things that could quietly lose work.

Pomodoro

  • A new Pomodoro page: a focus timer with short and long breaks, a ring that counts down, and a note of how many sessions you have finished today. Session lengths, rounds and auto-starting breaks are all adjustable on the page itself.
  • While a session runs, a small pill follows you around the app so the timer stays visible when you are writing. Click it to go back to the page.
  • Start, pause, reset and skip are in the command palette.
  • The timer keeps real time. Sending the app to the tray, closing the window or letting the Mac sleep no longer makes it drift.

12-hour and 24-hour time

  • Settings → General now has a time format choice, and everything that shows a clock time follows it: the calendar, reminders, routines, habits and backups. New installs use 24-hour.

Nothing gets lost

  • A locked note still called "Untitled" could be deleted by the app's own tidy-up. Locking deliberately blanks the text so it is not readable at rest, and the tidy-up read that as an empty note. Locked notes are now left alone.
  • Restoring a backup could, if it ran into a problem part-way, leave you with nothing: the old notes already cleared and the new ones not yet written. A restore is now all-or-nothing — if anything goes wrong, or the app is force-quit mid-restore, your existing notes are untouched.
  • A restore that could not use any of a backup's contents used to report success over an emptied app. It now refuses, and says what it could not read.
  • Writing a backup, exporting a note or saving to a linked file could leave a half-written file behind if the app stopped mid-write — including on top of a good backup. These are now written whole or not at all.
  • Editing one note and quickly switching to another could drop the first note's last edit.
  • When a save failed, nothing said so. Saves now retry on their own, and if they still cannot land you get a message with a Retry button. Your unsaved text is held on to rather than being replaced by the older copy on disk. A full disk says so plainly.

Fixes

  • The app leaked memory the whole time an image sat on the clipboard, and grew until it ran out. Copying a screenshot made this fast.
  • In a note with more than one table, the column buttons acted on the first table on the page instead of the one you were pointing at.
  • A routine's timer lost its progress when you closed the runner, and started the step again from the top.
  • Large code notes were slow to type in, and their highlighting quietly gave up past the first few thousand characters. Both are fixed; a several-megabyte file now types at full speed.
  • The code font setting had no effect on code notes.
  • In a confirmation dialog, moving to Cancel and pressing Return ran the action you were trying to refuse.
  • Option and the arrow keys jumped through history instead of moving the cursor a word at a time inside text.
  • A routine step given an invalid length silently became untimed. It now says so and will not save until it is fixed.
  • Attachments and pasted images are capped at 10 MB, with a clear message, instead of being accepted and slowing the note down.
